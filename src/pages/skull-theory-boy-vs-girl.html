Skull Theory: Boy vs Girl Skull Shape

Skull theory claims you can guess your baby's sex by the shape of its head on an early ultrasound — a round, smooth skull for a girl and a longer, squarer skull with a stronger brow for a boy.

Quick Answer

Enthusiasts say a square, longer skull with a prominent brow = boy and a round, smooth skull = girl, read around 12 weeks. But there is no scientific evidence it works: real differences in skull shape between males and females only develop around puberty, not in the womb. At 12 weeks a baby’s head shape is set by genetics and position, not sex. Skull theory performs at about chance.

What Skull Theory Claims to Look For

Followers compare a few features on a first-trimester scan:

  • Overall roundness — a rounder head is read as a girl.
  • Length — a longer, more oval head is read as a boy.
  • Brow and forehead — a flatter, more pronounced brow ridge is read as a boy.
  • Back of the skull — a flatter occiput is read as a boy, a rounder one as a girl.

When Skull Sex Differences Actually Develop

The problem with skull theory is timing. Male and female skulls genuinely do differ — men tend to have a more pronounced brow ridge, a squarer jaw and a flatter back of the head — but those differences are driven by sex hormones acting at puberty and later, and they are population averages, not reliable markers for any one person. A 12-week fetus has barely begun to ossify its skull, and its shape reflects genes, amniotic pressure and head position far more than sex.

Does Skull Theory Actually Work?

No peer-reviewed study has validated reading fetal sex from first-trimester skull shape. Where the idea has been tested, it lands at about chance. It also cannot be double-checked the way nub theory can, because there is no anatomical structure tied to sex that a head shape is meant to reveal. In short: it is the weakest of the popular ultrasound guesses.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is skull theory?

The claim that a baby's head shape on an early ultrasound reveals sex — round for a girl, square for a boy.

Does skull theory actually work?

No evidence supports it. Real skull differences appear around puberty, not in the womb, so a 12-week head shape is not a sex marker.

When do skull differences appear?

During and after puberty, driven by sex hormones — never early enough for a first-trimester scan to read.

Is nub theory better?

Yes. Nub theory reads a structure that actually differs by sex; skull theory does not.
